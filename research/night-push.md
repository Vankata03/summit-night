# The night summit push on the South Col route

Research for issue [#5](https://github.com/Vankata03/summit-night/issues/5). It gathers the facts the **headlamp train** chapter needs: when climbers leave and arrive, how many are on the route and how spread out they are, what the headlamps look like, the weather and moon, and when and where the sun rises at the summit during the main May season.

Each claim has its source next to it. A number marked **derived** is my own calculation or inference from cited data, not a figure published by the source.

## Timing

### Departures

Teams on the South Col route generally leave Camp IV between about 21:00 and midnight Nepal Time (UTC+5:45). Some leave earlier, around 19:30–20:00, to get ahead of the queues ([Alan Arnette, 2012 summit wave report](https://www.alanarnette.com/2012/05/24/everest-2012-summit-wave-4/); [Arnette, South Col route description](https://www.alanarnette.com/everest/everestsouthroutes.php)). Arnette's dispatches come from first-hand reporting and team updates. No dataset records departure times, so treat this window as practitioner consensus rather than measured data.

### Summit arrivals

The Himalayan Database is the authoritative record of Himalayan ascents. Its *Himalaya by the Numbers (1950–2019)*, Chart A-24d (p. 92), plots member ascents of Everest by hour of day for 1990–2019 ([PDF](https://www.himalayandatabase.org/downloads/HimalayaByNbrs%20(1950-2019).pdf)):

- South Col route: arrivals rise from about 03:00, peak in the 07:00–07:59 hour (~440 members), and drop off after 11:00. Roughly: 05:00 ~235, 06:00 ~370, 07:00 ~440, 08:00 ~385, 09:00 ~325, 10:00 ~250, 11:00 ~160 (read from the chart).
- The text on p. 89 gives the mean Everest ascent time as 06:00–10:00, far earlier than on other 8,000 m peaks, because of the overnight push.

**Derived:** a 21:00–23:00 departure and a 06:00–09:00 arrival give 8–10 hours of climbing. Most of the headlamp train is therefore in darkness, and the front of the train reaches the summit around sunrise.

### Waypoints, altitudes and the order of events

Station altitudes come from the Everest automatic weather station network ([Everest weather-station network paper, ESSD 2026](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/7253/2026/)): South Col 7,945 m, Balcony 8,430 m, Bishop Rock 8,810 m. The summit's official height is 8,848.86 m, from the 2020 Nepal–China survey.

A typical sequence (Arnette's route description, cross-checked against the arrival histogram above):

1. Leave the South Col, 21:00–23:00.
2. Climb the Triangular Face to the Balcony (~8,400 m), the first rest and oxygen change, about 4–5 h later.
3. Climb the southeast ridge to the South Summit (~8,750 m), around first light.
4. Cross the summit ridge and the Hillary Step (8,790 m; its rock step was altered in the 2015 earthquake), where queues form.
5. Reach the summit, 05:00–09:00, with the sun up.

## Crowding and spread

The Himalayan Database counts climbers by day, including hired staff such as Sherpas (*Himalaya by the Numbers*, Charts A-21a–c, pp. 88–89):

- Busiest South Col days, 2000–2019: in 2019 the two biggest days of 22 and 23 May had about 225 and 244 people above Base Camp, with ~219 and ~218 summits. Most other high-traffic days have 100–210 (e.g. 19 May 2016: 212 above BC, 207 ascents).
- For a "busy night", **use ~200–250 headlamps on the South Col route.** A typical good night has well under 100. Chart A-21a lists only days above 100, so the threshold itself shows that most nights are smaller.
- Both routes on one day: up to 419 above BC and 380 summits (22–23 May 2019, Chart A-21c).

**Spread (derived):** departures span 3–4 hours, and climbers gain roughly 100 vertical metres an hour. The train therefore spreads over 300–500 m of height at any moment, and climbers clip to the same fixed ropes in single file. On a busy night the line is almost unbroken from the Triangular Face to the ridge, with dense queues at the Balcony, the South Summit and the Hillary Step. Climbers in a queue stand 1–3 m apart; between queues the gaps run to tens of metres. On a quiet night the train breaks up into separate team clusters of 3–10 lights, with long dark gaps between them (cluster size **derived** from typical commercial teams of a few clients, each with a Sherpa).

## Headlamps

- Modern headlamps for the summit push are rated at about 300–1,500 lm at maximum. Petzl NAO RL, for example: 1,500 lm max, 200 m beam, plus a rear red light "to remain visible at night" ([Petzl NAO RL](https://www.petzl.com/INT/en/Sport/Headlamps/NAO-RL)).
- **Derived:** on summit night climbers run headlamps on low or medium settings (roughly 100–300 lm) to save batteries in the cold. Seen from a distance, each lamp is a small cool-white point. The beam pools on snow only within a few metres of the climber.
- Colour: white LEDs, which read as cool white (manufacturers rarely publish the colour temperature; 5,000–6,500 K is typical of white LEDs — **derived**). A few red rear lights add colour to the line.
- **Rendering implication:** the train reads as a dotted line of points with a faint glow, and short beam cones where the camera is close. It does not light the mountainside.

## Weather

- Summit nights fall inside short windows in May, when the subtropical jet stream lifts north off the Himalaya and summit winds drop ([Weather Underground, Christopher Burt](https://www.wunderground.com/blog/weatherhistorian/extreme-weather-the-weather-of-the-summit-of-mt-everest.html)). Teams wait for forecasts of summit wind below about 30 mph (~50 km/h) ([Arnette, 2026 jet stream update](https://www.alanarnette.com/2026/05/18/everest-2026-weather-alert-and-more-summits/)).
- Summit temperature in May averages about −25 to −27 °C (Burt). At the South Col station the mean ranges from −29.7 °C in February to −12.2 °C in July ([Everest weather-station network paper, ESSD 2026](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/18/7253/2026/)). The South Col has recorded winds above 30 m/s and gusts above 60 m/s, but not on summit nights.
- The monsoon reached the South Col on 14 June 2022 and 1 July 2019 (same paper), so the late-May window is pre-monsoon: usually clear above the cloud, with cloud and fog in the valleys below.
- **Rendering implication:** clear, star-filled sky above; valley fog below the route (this fits the "valley fog" atmosphere preference); a small snow plume, or none, on a summit night.

## Moon

The moon's phase on summit night varies from year to year, so it is a free artistic choice. Examples from the [USNO API](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/api) for the summit's coordinates:

| Night | Phase | Moon in the sky during the push |
|---|---|---|
| 22 May 2019 (record traffic) | Waning gibbous, 88% | rises 22:01, bright all night |
| 20 May 2026 | Waxing crescent, 17% | sets 22:46, dark sky after the first hour |
| 27 May 2026 | Waxing gibbous, 85% | up until 02:12 |

May 2026 phases (UT): full 1 May, new 16 May, first quarter 23 May, full 31 May.

**Recommendation:** a moonless sky, or a thin moon set early, so the headlamps and stars carry the scene. If the chapter wants moonlight on the snow, a waning gibbous rising mid-push matches the record night of 2019.

## Sunrise at the summit

I computed these for 27.9881° N, 86.9250° E, 8,849 m, in Nepal Time (UTC+5:45), with the NOAA/Meeus solar-position algorithm. The sea-level sunrise matches USNO's API to the minute (20 May 2026: 05:05).

The summit sees the sun earlier than sea level, because at 8,849 m the visible horizon drops about 3.0° below the horizontal (geometric dip). I set summit sunrise at a solar elevation of −3.46°: dip, plus atmospheric refraction scaled to ~0.31 atm, plus the sun's semi-diameter.

| Date | Astronomical twilight | Nautical twilight | Civil twilight (first light) | Summit sunrise (azimuth) | Sea-level sunrise (azimuth) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 May | 03:44 | 04:16 | 04:46 | **04:58** (68.0°) | 05:11 (69.5°) |
| 15 May | 03:40 | 04:12 | 04:42 | **04:55** (66.6°) | 05:08 (68.1°) |
| 20 May | 03:36 | 04:09 | 04:40 | **04:52** (65.2°) | 05:05 (66.8°) |
| 25 May | 03:33 | 04:06 | 04:37 | **04:50** (64.1°) | 05:03 (65.7°) |
| 31 May | 03:30 | 04:04 | 04:35 | **04:48** (63.0°) | 05:02 (64.5°) |

Solar noon is 11:54, when the sun stands 80–84° high. The year changes these times by under a minute.

- **Direction:** the sun rises at azimuth ~65°, east-north-east, over the Tibetan plateau beyond the Kangshung Face. Makalu lies at a bearing of about 124° from the summit (**derived** from peak coordinates), so the sun comes up well to the left of Makalu's silhouette when seen from the summit.
- **Order of light:** the sky starts to brighten around 03:35. By 04:40 there is enough light to see the ridge. The summit pyramid catches direct sun at about 04:50, while the lower route, 900 m down at the South Col, gets direct sun a few minutes later. This is the alpenglow moment where the chapter ends.
- **Train at sunrise (derived):** at 04:50 the leading climbers are near the South Summit or the Hillary Step, and most of the train is still on the Triangular Face and the Balcony. Arrivals peak an hour or two after sunrise.
